Branching Logic is an investigation into Colorado native plants as both subject matter and methodological guide. Working across textile craft, computational design, and local botany, the project asks: what can we understand about the innate logic of living systems when viewed through lenses of craft and computation? We orient our interdisciplinary practices toward discovery and curiosity, exploring plants of the Front Range through plant walks with botanists and permitted flower collection.

We focused on two plants from the aster family. The compound flower of the aster, many small flowers clustered into one larger flower, inspired a woven pattern built from small units tiled into a bigger design. The branching stems of rabbitbrush led us to grow L-systems into woven figures in AdaCAD. We also collected rabbitbrush blossoms to dye cotton, wool, and silk, testing how different mordants and after baths change the color.

We share this work on our project website, linked on this page, with step-by-step natural dyeing instructions, an interactive dyeing tool, and profiles of the plants we studied, and in a public exhibition at the Boulder Public Library in 2027. The project is supported by a NEST Studio for the Arts Graduate Summer Fellowship, awarded in partnership with SPIKE.

## Aster

The compound flower of the aster family, a cluster of small disk flowers encircled by petal-like ray flowers, inspired Etta to think about how woven pattern could be designed through a similar logic of clustered and stretched weave structures. She sketched a three-by-three cluster of twill pattern units, like the central disk flowers, with twill structures stretched by three pattern widths around its edges, like the rays.

The resulting design was cross-shaped, an isolated pattern with "empty" space around it, which raised a new question about how to bring it into an overall cloth design. Etta carved a stamp of the aster design in miniature to play with different ways of tiling it, then drafted the rectangular repeat in Photoshop to weave on the Jacquard loom. We are now building the aster logic into AdaCAD operations.

## Rabbitbrush

Rabbitbrush, a member of the aster family, forms a rounded crown of branching stems topped with small yellow flowers in late summer and fall. Deanna led an exploration of its branching through L-systems, a formal grammar for modeling plant growth, by creating an AdaCAD operation that grows an L-system and draws it as a woven figure. Her first sample (blue) varied branch thickness across generations, and the second (black) gave each branching depth its own structure, stepping from a dense satin at the trunk to finer twills at the twigs, so the third-generation twigs separate into distinct shapes instead of merging.

![Woven L-system samples in blue and black, with close-ups](/img/branching-logic-lsystem-samples.jpg)

We also collected rabbitbrush blossoms under a Boulder County permit at Hall Ranch in Lyons and used them to dye cotton, wool, and silk, which produced a butter yellow on silk and cotton and a bright gold on wool.
